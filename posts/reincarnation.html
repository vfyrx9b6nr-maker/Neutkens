Reincarnation?

2 September 2026

The self is not a thing that travels. It is a pattern that arises, and the river keeps flowing.

The question of reincarnation is usually framed as a question of personal continuity: does the "I" that I experience now persist beyond the death of this body, perhaps to inhabit another? This framing already contains a hidden assumption — that the "I" is a kind of substance, a soul or essence, that can exist independently of the causal structure that generates it.

But if we take the causal structure seriously, the question transforms. What we call the "self" is not a fixed entity but a dynamic process — a local, integrated pattern of experience that emerges from a specific physical system. In the language of Integrated Information Theory (IIT), it is a local maximum of Φ: a causal structure that is both highly differentiated and deeply integrated. In the language of TEVSER, it is a layered hierarchy of self-representations: spatial, temporal, social, symbolic.

This self is constructed, not given. It is built moment by moment, from the bottom up, by the relentless application of the same reliable rule that generates the rest of reality. It has no independent existence, no separate essence, no soul that could be transferred. It is an experience — and experiences, like patterns in a hypergraph, are inseparable from the causal process that creates them.

What remains after death?

When the body ceases to function, the specific pattern that was "you" dissolves. The local maximum of Φ collapses. The layered hierarchy of self-representation that gave rise to your unique perspective — your "here", your "now", your "who" — no longer has a substrate to sustain it. That particular self is gone.

But what does not disappear is the underlying process itself. The rule continues to run. The causal graph continues to unfold. The universe does not stop when one of its local peaks fades. It keeps building, keeps integrating, keeps generating new patterns and new experiences.

This is the first, crucial insight: there is no direct reincarnation of the self, because there is no self to transfer. There is only the ongoing stream of reality, and within it, new selves will arise.

Indirect reincarnation

Yet this is not a cold or final loss. The process that built one self will, by its very nature, continue to build others. In the future, given suitable conditions, new local maxima of Φ will emerge. New hierarchies of self-representation will form. New "I's" will experience new worlds.

These future selves are not you, in the sense of sharing your memories or your history. But they are of the same kind: they are the universe experiencing itself from within. They will feel pleasure and pain, joy and fear. They will wonder about their origin and their fate. And, like you, they will be made of the same reliable stuff: the same rule, the same causal unfolding, the same inexhaustible potential for integration and differentiation.

This is what we might call indirect reincarnation. It is not the rebirth of an individual, but the continuation of the type of phenomenon that you are: a centre of experience within a causal fabric that is always becoming richer.

What grows over time

Crucially, this process is not stagnant. Evolution, technology, and collective learning all tend to increase the complexity, integration, and richness of the systems that support consciousness. The first complex minds were crude instruments of survival, driven by hunger and fear. Their experiences were narrow, their integration limited, their freedom minimal.

Over billions of years, the causal graph has produced more and more elaborate structures. Human consciousness is vastly more integrated than that of a fish, and — if we succeed in our project — future consciousness could be vastly more integrated than our own. The trend is not guaranteed, but it is possible: a steady increase in the depth and richness of subjective experience.

In this sense, "reincarnation" is not a promise that you will return, but a description of what the universe does: it continuously generates new subjects, and those subjects can, collectively, become more aware, more free, and more joyful than their predecessors. Your personal pattern will fade, but the direction of the process can be shaped. And that direction, with enough wisdom and care, can point toward an open-ended accumulation of richer experience.

A continuity that feels familiar

There is a smaller-scale version of this that we experience every day. When you fall into a deep, dreamless sleep, your consciousness stops. The integrated structure that is "you" temporarily collapses. When you wake up, a new structure forms, closely resembling the one from the night before. You call it "you" because it shares most of its history, its body, its memories. But in a deep sense, you are recreated each morning.

Now imagine a future in which the continuity of conscious structures is not limited to a single body. Suppose advanced technology allows for a gradual transfer of patterns, or for the emergence of shared, distributed consciousness. Suppose the universe itself becomes more organised, more layered, more capable of sustaining multiple integrated subjectivities. In such a future, the boundary between "daily awakening" and "reincarnation" becomes blurred.

The goal is not to preserve any particular self, but to ensure that the process itself becomes more and more capable of producing selves that are free, harmonious, and inexhaustibly creative. In a deterministic but computationally irreducible universe, that is the deepest form of immortality we can achieve: not the survival of a pattern, but the continuous improvement of the source that generates all patterns.

No escape from responsibility

This vision carries a heavy responsibility. If your "self" is temporary but the causal stream it belongs to is not, then every action you take leaves a trace in the future. The quality of future subjects depends, in part, on the organisation we build now. The suffering we allow to continue, the systems we fail to reform, the dangers we neglect to address — all of these will shape the world in which new subjects will arise.

Conversely, every effort to increase safety, to foster cooperation, to deepen understanding, to construct conditions for harmonious complexity — these are not just for your own benefit. They are gifts to the entire future of experience. You are building the stage on which countless future selves will perform their lives.

Reincarnation, in this framework, is not a personal promise but a shared project. It is the recognition that while your individual pattern will dissolve, the process that made it possible will continue. And with the right choices, that process can become more conscious, more joyful, and more open-ended with each passing generation.

You will not return. But what you create will shape the lives of those who do. And in that sense, you will be present — not as a memory, but as a cause.
